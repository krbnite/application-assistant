# Cover Letter Bank

This file holds reusable narrative material for cover letters: factual renderings, interpretation, and motivation. Select and rewrite it around the current reader and argument. Canonical accomplishment facts and claim boundaries live in RESUME_BULLET_BANK.md, not in historical letters or the smoothest paragraph here.

Use old cover letters as style references only. Do not treat any prior company-specific cover letter as canonical source text.

## Use Rules

The accomplishment bank is the factual authority. This file supplies optional narrative renderings, interpretation, and motivation; it is not a second record of Kevin's history. Read the relevant source entries and their evidence/boundaries before drafting factual claims. The source register below is a locator, not a substitute for that read.

Choose the reader's main uncertainty and one central argument before selecting paragraphs. Research Scientist, Research Engineer / Applied Scientist, and Technical Lead are optional contribution lenses, not fixed letter types or required sections. Domain clusters help locate relevant material; they do not prescribe the argument.

Select two or three examples that establish the argument. Give each paragraph a distinct purpose. An opening may already establish the company connection; do not add a separate bridge to repeat it. Use a short, concrete closing. Existing module IDs remain available for compatibility, not as a recommended checklist.

Write fresh or combine/split prose whenever it improves the letter and the facts remain supported. Do not force a paragraph into the letter because it is already polished. Canonical wording is a useful starting point, not a voice template.

Ordinary letters usually fit one page in roughly 300–450 words; this is a starting range, not a floor or quota. Text boxes follow their actual limits. A longer research-interest letter needs a reason. Cut repetition before changing typography.

### Selecting the argument

- Research Scientist: make a modeling question, experimental choice, contribution and interpretation visible. Publications support that evidence; they do not replace it.
- Research Engineer / Applied Scientist: connect a real user or scientific problem to an owned artifact, a correctness/integration decision and a usable result.
- Technical Lead: show an agenda, priority, interface or quality decision, then distinguish personal and delegated work and explain the team/product result. Titles do not establish operating scale.
- Combine lenses when useful, with one dominant argument. Measurement science and business-facing ML are useful variations, not mandatory new clusters.

### Voice and selection

Keep Kevin's curiosity, technical precision and interest in how systems work. Prefer a concrete observation, choice or intellectual attraction to declarations that he is rigorous or a strong fit. Ask whether he could comfortably say and defend the sentence; do not mistake polished corporate prose for his voice. Do not invent motivation or force a personal sentence into every letter. AI-detector scores are not an acceptance test.

Avoid recurring inventories of modalities, methods and failure modes. Select the details that changed the scientific or engineering decision. Do not routinely repeat the company mission in the opening, bridge and closing. Generic phrases such as "compelling extension," "at the intersection," or "adjacent depth" should not carry the argument.

Use a qualification boundary only when omitting it would create a consequential false impression. Often the accurate verb—proposed, prototyped, directed, implemented—supplies the boundary. If a gap needs stating, keep it short and specific; do not follow it with a second skills inventory. A cover letter cannot remove an actual must-have gap by analogy.

Keep historical letters intact unless Kevin requests revisions. Prior letters are editorial examples, not new evidence. Promote reusable insights only after checking the canonical sources; record unconfirmed claims for clarification rather than copying or silently declaring them false.

### Generator boundary

Only single-paragraph renderings belong in the renderable section at the end of this file. The current generator treats everything under a module heading as rendered prose; internal notes must remain above that section in the source register or in plan metadata. Editorial planning fields are not automatically validated by the generator and require the writer's review. Existing plans with plain module IDs remain supported.

### EVID-CVB-01: Wearable digital biomarker evidence

I helped choose IMU sampling rates using movement bandwidth, instrument noise, and 25–400 Hz ablations. ### EVID-CVB-02: Responsible clinical ML judgment evidence

At Cohen Veterans Bioscience, I evaluated a wearable suicide-risk prediction initiative as infeasible without an adequate prospective data foundation and redirected work toward more tractable clinical risk indicators. ### EVID-CVB-03: Sensor-driven questionnaire estimation evidence

At Cohen Veterans Bioscience, I conducted a literature review and proposed estimating established clinical assessments from passive sensing as a more tractable alternative to a wearable-suicide-prediction mandate. ### EVID-POD-04: Adaptive estimation and pressure fusion

For SmartMat+, I combined four pressure sensors into center-of-pressure trajectories and scalar sway speed, then developed streaming fall-risk estimators. Adaptive estimation reduced the time to reliable patient-specific estimates from roughly 20–30 days to 3–5 days. I translated the selected Python estimator to C, checked numerical equivalence, and supported firmware integration for prospective testing.

### EVID-POD-05: Hardware adaptation and release

I adapted SmartMat's thermal-processing algorithms for SmartMat+, addressing changes in sensor geometry, placement, materials, and manufacturing through device-specific processing and calibration. Working with hardware and software engineers, that adaptation enabled the second-generation device's release. It required tracing which changes in the measurements came from the hardware and what the algorithms needed to accommodate.

### EVID-POD-06: Thermal physics and synthetic data

At Podimetrics, I developed physics-informed thermal models and a synthetic thermogram framework to investigate spatiotemporal sensor behavior. A Newtonian heat-transfer model extrapolated short scans toward thermal equilibrium. Simulation gave me a way to reason about sparse measurements and examine conditions that algorithm development and evaluation needed to cover.

### EVID-CVB-04: Reusable scientific software

After repeatedly implementing related biosignal models, I developed SensorNet, an internal pip-installable package with configurable Conv1D-LSTM models, training and inference methods, and time-series utilities. Projects installed documented revisions as the package evolved. I also reviewed researchers' packages and modeling pipelines for implementation choices and leakage risks that could change the scientific conclusion.

### EVID-CVB-05: Funded research direction

At Cohen Veterans Bioscience, I named, built, and led the Sensor Analytics Team and originated the vision, technical plan, and roadmap for longitudinal Parkinson's monitoring using Verily Watch data from PPMI. ### EVID-CVB-06: Experiment authorship and attribution

At Cohen Veterans Bioscience, I conceived systematic ablations comparing an end-to-end Conv1D-LSTM approach with a classical signal-processing, handcrafted-feature, and SVM pipeline. I delegated most implementation to a direct report and personally contributed the final, best-performing architecture. The experiments tested which parts of the learned approach accounted for the improvement across the biosignal tasks we studied.

### EVID-CVB-07: Measurement and evaluation decisions

In a gesture-recognition prototype, I found overlapping-window leakage, time-ordering bias, and an evaluation target that did not match the intended use. I redesigned validation around unseen-patient generalization. In separate sleep work, I developed a DTW-based agreement framework to account for physiological timing differences between EEG- and motion-based measurements. Both cases required examining what the evaluation was measuring before interpreting a model's score.

### EVID-WWE-02: Measured modeling contribution

At WWE, I developed churn models reaching approximately 0.77 AUROC through new behavioral and calendar features, input transformations, and experiments with deeper neural networks and Gaussian-noise regularization between hidden layers.

### EVID-WWE-03: Data delivery and business outcomes

At WWE, I built API-based collection pipelines and co-developed live-event reporting that brought metrics to stakeholders within minutes, replacing manual processes that took 12–24 hours. Related data and reporting services had cost roughly $1M annually. I also maintained automated model-scoring workflows that wrote churn predictions to Redshift for marketing and product dashboards.

### EVID-CSTR-02: Testing an accepted representation

In my doctoral research, I used median-relative spectral analysis over long Antarctic magnetometer baselines to test whether standard magnetic coordinates correctly represented high-latitude observations. The analysis revealed polar-cap structure obscured in short event studies and showed how coordinate choices could misclassify sensor sites. An accepted representation became a hypothesis to test against the measurements.

### EVID-MSG-01: Independent AI workflow and evaluation

In independent work, I built ProblemForm, a human-AI problem-formulation system with a multi-phase LLM refinement pipeline, CLI, benchmarks, and rubric/property-based evaluation. The system refines questions, plans, and specifications before downstream execution and makes the proposed changes available for evaluation.

### EVID-MSG-02: Simulation and learned approximations

My simulation work began with translating analytical granular-dynamics models into numerical simulations and visualizations, contributing to peer-reviewed publications. In a later independent collaboration, I worked with mechanical-engineering researchers on RNN forecasting and PyTorch surrogate-modeling approaches for expensive tapped-granular-system simulations. Those projects connect physical models with learned approximations while keeping their different assumptions visible.
